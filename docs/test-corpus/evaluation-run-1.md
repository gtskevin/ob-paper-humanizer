## T1: Introduction (AMJ 2015, Baer et al.)

### T1-A: Human Original (148 words)

There is a clear consensus that trusting—being willing to accept vulnerability to the actions of another party based on positive expectations about their attributes (Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, 1995)—is a beneficial component of work relationships (Dirks & Ferrin, 2001; Gambetta, 1988; Rousseau, Sitkin, Burt, & Camerer, 1998). Meta-analyses have reinforced this consensus, finding that employees who trust their supervisors tend to have better job performance, more frequent citizenship behavior, and higher job satisfaction (Colquitt, Scott, & LePine, 2007; Dirks & Ferrin, 2002). That same consensus extends to research addressing feeling trusted—the perception that another party is willing to accept vulnerability to one's actions. Scholars have suggested that placing trust in employees is a signal to them that they are valued (Pfeffer, 1998)—a key to employee empowerment and engagement (Kahn, 1990; Mishra & Mishra, 2012) and a foundational element of high-involvement workplaces (Lawler, 1992).

**Key OB conventions present**: Funnel structure (broad→narrow→gap), hedging ("scholars have suggested"), meta-analytic citation pattern.

It is important to note that trust plays a crucial role in organizational functioning. Trust, defined as the willingness to accept vulnerability based on positive expectations of another party (Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, 1995), has gained significant attention in the literature as a vital component of work relationships (Dirks & Ferrin, 2001; Gambetta, 1988; Rousseau, Sitkin, Burt, & Camerer, 1998). Furthermore, meta-analyses have demonstrated that employees who trust their supervisors tend to exhibit better job performance, more frequent citizenship behavior, and higher job satisfaction (Colquitt, Scott, & LePine, 2007; Dirks & Ferrin, 2002). Moreover, this consensus extends to research addressing feeling trusted—the perception that another party is willing to accept vulnerability to one's actions. Importantly, scholars have suggested that placing trust in employees signals that they are valued (Pfeffer, 1998), which is a key driver of employee empowerment and engagement (Kahn, 1990; Mishra & Mishra, 2012) and a foundational element of high-involvement workplaces (Lawler, 1992).

## T2: Theory/Hypotheses (JAP 2016, Wellman et al.)

### T2-A: Human Original (165 words)

Building on role theory, we propose that actors who engage in moral objection are more likely to meet observers' role expectations when they are high rather than low in legitimate power. Moral objection involves explicitly questioning the ethicality of a commonly accepted practice or task, and refusing to go along with the practice. Thus, it is an instantiation of the agentic (i.e., confident, independent, change-oriented) behavior that is widely expected of high-power individuals. When individuals high in legitimate power engage in moral objection, their behavior is likely to meet observers' role expectations in that the objector will be seen as confidently encouraging the group to adopt higher ethical standards. In keeping with this argument, research has found that employees expect formal leaders to be a significant source of ethical guidance in the workplace (Brown, Trevino, & Harrison, 2005; Okimoto & Wenzel, 2009; Trevino, 1992; Trevino & Ball, 1992). When individuals low in legitimate power engage in moral objection, however, this is likely to violate observers' role expectations.

**Key OB conventions present**: "We propose" stance-taking, appropriate hedging ("are more likely to", "is likely to"), theory-driven argumentation.

### T2-B: AI-Rewritten Version (test input)

Building on role theory, it can be argued that actors who engage in moral objection may potentially be more likely to meet observers' role expectations when they are high rather than low in legitimate power. Moral objection involves explicitly questioning the ethicality of a commonly accepted practice or task, and refusing to go along with the practice. This is an instantiation of the agentic behavior (i.e., confident, independent, change-oriented) that is widely expected of high-power individuals. It is worth noting that when individuals high in legitimate power engage in moral objection, their behavior may possibly meet observers' role expectations, as the objector will be seen as confidently encouraging the group to adopt higher ethical standards. Additionally, research has demonstrated that employees expect formal leaders to be a crucial source of ethical guidance in the workplace (Brown, Trevino, & Harrison, 2005; Okimoto & Wenzel, 2009; Trevino, 1992; Trevino & Ball, 1992). Conversely, when individuals low in legitimate power engage in moral objection, this is likely to violate observers' role expectations.

**AI patterns injected**: Hedging overload ("may potentially be more likely"), "It can be argued" (removes "we propose" stance), hollow framing ("It is worth noting that"), transition monotony ("Additionally... Conversely"), "crucial" inflation, "demonstrated" vs "found".

## T4: Discussion (AMJ 2020, Grijalva et al.)

### T4-A: Human Original (102 words)

We demonstrated that teams with higher levels of narcissism tend to have poorer coordination, which in turn curbs team performance. Further, the costs of having one or multiple narcissists on a team increases as team members become more familiar with one another. Teams with higher narcissism levels are unable to capitalize on the benefits usually associated with getting to know one's teammates better. Thus, our findings suggest that there may be some truth to the old adage, "There is no 'I' in team"—at least, not if the team needs to maintain functional working relationships in highly interdependent contexts over time.

**Key OB conventions present**: Strong scholarly voice ("We demonstrated"), specific verb ("curbs"), evaluative language ("unable to capitalize"), memorable closing.

### T4-B: AI-Rewritten Version (test input)

The findings of this study demonstrate that teams with higher levels of narcissism tend to exhibit poorer coordination, which in turn undermines team performance. Moreover, the costs associated with having one or multiple narcissists on a team increase significantly as team members become more familiar with one another. Importantly, teams with higher narcissism levels are unable to capitalize on the benefits that are typically associated with getting to know one's teammates better. Consequently, these results suggest that there may be some validity to the well-known adage, "There is no 'I' in team"—particularly in contexts where teams need to maintain functional working relationships in highly interdependent settings over extended periods of time.

**AI patterns injected**: "The findings of this study demonstrate" (removes "We"), transition monotony ("Moreover... Importantly... Consequently"), generic verb substitution ("undermines" vs specific "curbs"), "significantly" inflation, verbose padding ("associated with", "that are typically", "over extended periods of time").
